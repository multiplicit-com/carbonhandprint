---
title: Nokia and the handprint of connectivity
description: One of the first companies to help build the carbon handprint method now puts it at the centre of its strategy.
---

If you want to see how far the handprint idea has travelled, look at Nokia.

## A founding partner of the method

Between 2016 and 2018, [VTT](https://www.vttresearch.com/) and LUT University in Finland worked with a group of companies to develop a way of measuring a product's positive climate impact. Nokia was one of them, alongside Neste, KONE, Gasum and others. The result, the [Carbon Handprint Guide](https://sarjaweb.vtt.fi/julkaisut/muut/2018/Carbon_Handprint_Guide.pdf), was published in December 2018. It defines a product's carbon handprint as the reduction in a customer's emissions compared with a baseline, and puts it simply: "the bigger the handprint, the better" ([announcement](https://phys.org/news/2018-12-environmental-indicator-positive-climate-impacts.html)).

## Handprint as strategy

Since then Nokia has made the handprint part of how it describes itself. Its sustainability pages talk about the benefits of connectivity as ["the handprint of digitalization"](https://www.nokia.com/about-us/sustainability/all-about-zero/), and its ESG strategy is framed as five areas chosen ["to maximize our handprint and minimize our footprint"](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-T/climatechange/symposia/202405/Documents/Anthony%20Darcy.pdf).

The handprint examples Nokia gives include [private wireless networks](https://www.bell-labs.com/blog/understanding-measuring-and-reporting-our-impact-on-the-environment) that help factories and ports run more efficiently, connected agriculture that can raise yields while using less water and energy, and digital twins. Its 2025 [climate transition plan](https://www.nokia.com/sites/default/files/2025-06/climate-transition-plan.pdf) also lists network equipment that cuts energy use for its operator customers, and a calculator that lets businesses assess the environmental impact of private wireless.

## Why the footprint still matters

Nokia is a useful example of why the two measures belong together. More than 95% of its emissions come from its products being used, which means the energy its customers' networks consume. So the same piece of kit sits on both sides of the ledger. A more efficient radio shrinks Nokia's footprint *and* grows its handprint, because the operator running it uses less electricity.

Nokia has also set a science-based target to halve its emissions by 2030 against 2019 and to reach net zero by 2040. That's the right order of things: an honest footprint and a credible plan to shrink it, with the handprint alongside it rather than instead of it.

## What it means for the rest of us

You don't need to be a multinational to think this way. The question Nokia asks of its products, "who else's emissions go down because of what we do?", works just as well for a household, a school or a small business. That's your handprint.
