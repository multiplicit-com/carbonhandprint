title: Is all carbon capture equal?
description: Carbon capture, carbon removal and carbon offsets get talked about as if they were the same thing. They aren't. Here is how to tell a solid claim from a weak one.

"Carbon capture" has become a phrase that covers almost anything. A coal plant with a filter on its chimney, a machine that pulls CO₂ out of the air, a newly planted forest and a carbon credit bought online can all be described as capturing carbon. Some of them make a real, lasting difference. Some make much less difference than the label suggests.

We think it's worth knowing the difference, whether you're a customer looking at a "carbon neutral" label or an organisation deciding where to spend money.

## Three different things

**Carbon capture and storage (CCS)** catches CO₂ at the source, such as a power station, cement works or gas plant, before it reaches the air, and pumps it underground. At best it stops new emissions. It doesn't take any carbon back out of the atmosphere, and it never catches all of it.

**Carbon dioxide removal** takes CO₂ that is already in the air and locks it away. That includes planting and restoring forests, storing carbon in soils, direct air capture machines, burning plants for energy and storing the CO₂ (known as BECCS), biochar and spreading crushed rock that absorbs CO₂. Removal is the only kind of capture that can make up for emissions that have already happened.

**Carbon offsets or credits** are certificates that say a tonne of CO₂ was avoided or removed somewhere else. They can be backed by either of the above, but most have been backed by projects that claim to *avoid* emissions, such as protecting a forest that might otherwise have been cut down, or handing out efficient cookstoves.

## Capture at source: useful, but check the numbers

CCS may well be needed for industries like cement, where CO₂ is released by the chemistry itself and there's no simple alternative. But promised capture rates and real ones are often far apart.

Boundary Dam, a coal plant in Saskatchewan, is one example. It was promoted as capturing up to 90% of its emissions. Counting the flue gas that bypasses the unit, it has averaged about 57% ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/1.7191867)).

There is also the question of what happens to the CO₂ afterwards. For decades, much of the CO₂ captured around the world has been pumped into oil fields to push out more oil, known as enhanced oil recovery. The International Energy Agency has pointed out that this can only count as lower-carbon oil in narrow circumstances, such as when the CO₂ comes from the air or from plants, and that a stored tonne "can only be counted once" ([IEA](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/can-co2-eor-really-provide-carbon-negative-oil)).

## Removal: needed, but still tiny

Most scenarios for meeting the Paris goals need several billion tonnes of CO₂ to be removed from the air every year by mid-century. According to the [State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2024-06-05-new-report-states-7-9-billion-tonnes-co2-must-be-sustainably-removed-year-hit) led by the University of Oxford, about 2 billion tonnes a year is removed today, almost all of it by forests. Newer methods such as direct air capture remove about 1.3 million tonnes a year, less than 0.1% of the total.

The best-known direct air capture company, Climeworks, shows how hard scaling up is. Its first plant in Iceland has never reached 1,000 tonnes of net removals in a year, and its larger plant captured 750 tonnes in its first ten months against a design capacity of 36,000 tonnes a year ([Swissinfo](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/climate-solutions/why-swiss-carbon-removal-start-up-climeworks-faces-serious-headwinds/89386618)).

Removal is still worth pursuing. It is early, expensive and small, and it can't yet stand in for cutting emissions.

## Offsets: the weakest link

Offsets have had the roughest scrutiny. A review of studies covering more than 2,000 projects, published in Nature Communications in 2024, found that less than 16% of the credits issued represented real emission reductions ([Max Planck Society](https://www.mpg.de/23737687/climate-impact-of-carbon-crediting-projects-substantially-overestimated)). A 2023 investigation by the Guardian and others into rainforest credits from Verra, the largest certifier, reached similar conclusions, which Verra disputed.

The problem is rarely fraud. The benefit depends on a guess about what would have happened without the project, and those guesses have often been far too generous. The EU is now banning claims that a product is "climate neutral" or has a reduced impact because its emissions were offset ([European Parliament](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20240112IPR16772/meps-adopt-new-law-banning-greenwashing-and-misleading-product-information)).

## What to look out for

Whether you're reading a label or writing a sustainability plan, the same questions sort the strong claims from the weak ones.

1. **Is it capture, removal or a credit?** Only removal takes carbon back out of the air. Capture at source reduces new emissions. A credit is only as good as the project behind it.
2. **How much is actually captured?** Look for measured tonnes, not design capacity or "up to" figures.
3. **Where does the carbon go, and for how long?** Rock storage can last thousands of years. A forest can burn or be cut down. CO₂ used to produce more oil is a different thing again.
4. **Would it have happened anyway?** If a forest was never really at risk, protecting it on paper saves nothing.
5. **Is it counted once?** The same tonne shouldn't appear in two companies' accounts, or in a company's and a country's.
6. **Is it instead of cutting emissions, or as well as?** The strongest approach cuts emissions first and uses removals only for what genuinely can't be cut yet.

### For the public

Be wary of "carbon neutral", "net zero" or "climate positive" labels on individual products, especially if the small print mentions offsets. A company that says how much it has cut its own emissions, and backs it with published figures, is telling you more than one that says it has offset them.

### For organisations

Cut your own emissions first and report them openly. If you buy credits or removals, say exactly what they are, where they come from and how long the carbon is stored, and describe them as a contribution to climate action. Avoid presenting them as cancelling out your footprint. Favour removals that store carbon for a long time, and avoid neutrality claims that rely on offsets, which are already restricted in the EU.

## Why this matters for the handprint

A carbon handprint is about real reductions you help bring about. It has the same weak spots as an offset: the difference you claim depends on what would have happened otherwise, and the same tonne mustn't be counted twice. The questions above are worth asking of any handprint claim too, including our own.
