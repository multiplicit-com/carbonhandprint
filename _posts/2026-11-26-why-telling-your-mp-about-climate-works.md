title: Why telling your MP about climate actually works
description: Writing to your MP or councillor can feel pointless. The evidence says it isn't, and that how you say it matters as much as what you say.

On our [Paris Agreement page](/paris-agreement/) we say that telling your MP or councillor you want stronger climate plans is one of the most useful things anyone can do. That can sound like a nice sentiment with nothing behind it. One email, sent into an inbox that gets hundreds a week. Why would it change anything?

Here is why we think it does, and why the tone of the message makes a real difference.

## Politicians are guessing what you think

Most people assume their MP knows where the public stands on climate. The research says they often don't.

In August 2026, researchers at the University of Cambridge [published a study](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/uk-politicians-think-public-opposition-to-climate-policy-is-far-greater-than-it-is-study-finds) comparing what 100 sitting MPs believed about public opinion with what a large Ipsos poll actually found. MPs consistently overestimated how much opposition there was to climate policies. For grants to insulate homes, MPs thought around 18% of people were opposed. The real figure was 6%. On average they underestimated public support by about 11 percentage points, and they thought voters of different parties were twice as divided as they really are.

The authors point to one likely cause: politicians hear disproportionately from the loudest voices, in the media and online, and those voices don't reflect the full range of opinion. They also found that MPs who thought their policies were unpopular were less willing to speak up for them.

The same pattern turns up elsewhere. A study of senior staff in US congressional offices, published in the [American Political Science Review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/legislative-staff-and-representation-in-congress/D7735FCF39B843B9F3269FD39362FD66) in 2019, found they misjudged their own constituents' views on issues including regulating carbon dioxide. The staff who met most often with corporate lobby groups had the [most skewed picture](https://magazine.columbia.edu/article/how-congress-misreads-public-opinion). When ordinary constituents are quiet, other people fill the gap.

Globally, the gap is just as wide. A survey of nearly 130,000 people in 125 countries, published in [Nature Climate Change](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-024-01925-3) in 2024, found that 89% want more political action on climate. People everywhere underestimated how many others felt the same.

In the UK, the government's own [Public Attitudes Tracker](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-summer-2026/desnz-public-attitudes-tracker-net-zero-and-climate-change-summer-2026-uk) found in summer 2026 that 79% of people are concerned about climate change. If your MP thinks that support is thin, a message from a real constituent is a correction they need.

## When representatives learn what people think, they act on it

There is direct evidence that this information changes how representatives vote. In 2008, political scientists surveyed more than 10,000 people in New Mexico about the state budget and sent the results to a randomly chosen half of the state's legislators. Those who received their district's results were [much more likely to vote in line with their constituents](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps11-016) than those who didn't. Nothing else was different. They simply knew more about what the people they represent wanted.

A letter from you does a small version of the same thing. It tells your representative that someone in their area cares, and it gives them a reason to act.

## Where your message goes

Writing to your MP isn't the same as shouting into the void. Constituent correspondence is a core part of an MP's job. According to the [House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/casework/a-guide-to-casework/), MPs act on what they hear by speaking to government officials, writing to ministers, or raising issues in Parliament. Your letter can become a written question, a point in a debate, a conversation with a minister, or a vote cast with a bit more confidence.

Councillors matter just as much, and are often overlooked. The government's climate advisers, the Climate Change Committee, [found](https://www.theccc.org.uk/publication/local-authorities-and-the-sixth-carbon-budget/) that local authorities have powers or influence over roughly a third of emissions in their area: transport, planning, housing, waste and the council's own buildings. Councillors are also far easier to reach than MPs, and a handful of thoughtful messages can be noticed on a council in a way that would be lost in Westminster.

## Why a personal message works better

Not every message carries the same weight. Parliament's own [advice on contacting your MP](https://www.parliament.uk/get-involved/contact-an-mp-or-lord/contact-your-mp/) is to write to your own MP, include your address so they know you live in their constituency, and follow up if you haven't heard back in a couple of weeks. It also says that mass mailings to every MP "will not get their attention".

In the US, a survey of congressional staff by the Congressional Management Foundation found that around nine in ten said an individually written letter or email would influence an undecided member, while more than half believed most identical campaign messages are sent without the constituent really knowing about them ([CMF](https://www.congressfoundation.org/news/press-releases/333-congressional-staff-believe-constituents-are-more-influential-than-lobbyists)). A template can still be a useful starting point. Putting it into your own words, and adding why it matters to you, is what turns it into a message someone reads properly.

## Why respect matters

The people who read your message are human, and many of them are under real pressure. The Local Government Association runs a campaign called [Debate Not Hate](https://www.local.gov.uk/about/campaigns/debate-not-hate) because 7 in 10 councillors reported experiencing abuse or intimidation in a single year. It warns that this is putting people off standing for election at all.

An angry or abusive message is easy to set aside, and it adds to a climate that drives good people out of public life. A polite, specific message from a constituent is the opposite. It is hard to ignore, it can be quoted, and it gives a representative who already agrees with you something to point to. It also helps the ones who don't yet agree to change their mind without losing face.

Being respectful doesn't mean being vague. You can be clear and firm about what you want while assuming the person reading it is acting in good faith.

## What a good message looks like

- **Write to your own representative.** Find your MP on [Parliament's website](https://www.parliament.uk/get-involved/contact-an-mp-or-lord/contact-your-mp/) and your councillors on [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/find-your-local-councillors). Include your full address.
- **Keep it short and specific.** One issue, one clear ask. "Please support the council's plan for safe cycle routes to schools" is easier to act on than "do more about climate change".
- **Say why it matters to you.** A sentence about your street, your children's school, your bills or your work is worth more than a page of statistics.
- **Be polite, and thank them** for anything they have already done.
- **Ask a question** that needs a reply, such as how they intend to vote, or what they will do next.
- **Follow up.** If you get a reply, answer it. If you don't, write again or ask to meet at a surgery.

It takes about fifteen minutes. It's also one of the clearest examples of a carbon handprint we know: a small action of yours that can shift decisions affecting thousands of other people's emissions. For more ideas, see [Grow your handprint](/grow-your-handprint/).
