Climate news assumes you already know the vocabulary. A report mentions "Kyoto" or "Paris", someone says a country is "off track for 1.5", a summit is called "COP31", and nobody stops to explain. Here is what the most common references actually mean, roughly in the order they happened.

## Rio, 1992: where it started

At the [Earth Summit](/events/earth-summit-1992/) in Rio de Janeiro in 1992, countries signed the **United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change**, usually shortened to the **UNFCCC**. It is the treaty underneath everything else on this page. Its aim is to stop greenhouse gases building up to a level that would cause "dangerous" interference with the climate.

The Convention set up the process, but it didn't set binding targets. Almost every country in the world has signed up: there are 198 Parties ([UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/united-nations-framework-convention-on-climate-change)).

## COP: the yearly summit

**COP** stands for Conference of the Parties. It is the annual meeting of every country that has signed the Convention, and the number tells you which one it is. The first was in Berlin in 1995. COP21 was Paris in 2015, and COP31 was held in Antalya, Türkiye, in November 2026.

COPs are where the treaties below were agreed, and where the detailed rules for them are worked out. Decisions are made by consensus, so a single country can hold things up. That's why COPs often overrun and end with compromise wording. We keep a page on [the major climate talks](/events/), with what each one agreed.

## Kyoto, 1997: the first targets

The **Kyoto Protocol** was agreed at [COP3](/events/cop3/) in Kyoto, Japan, in 1997. It was the first deal with legally binding emission targets, and it applied only to industrialised countries. Together they had to cut emissions by an average of 5% below 1990 levels over 2008 to 2012 ([UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-kyoto-protocol)).

Kyoto took until 2005 to come into force. The United States never ratified it, and fast-growing economies such as China and India had no targets under it. When people say "Kyoto", they usually mean this model: a short list of rich countries with targets handed down from the top. It was a start, but it covered a shrinking share of global emissions.

## Copenhagen, 2009: the summit that disappointed

[COP15](/events/cop15/) in Copenhagen was meant to agree a successor to Kyoto that included every major emitter. It ended without one. The leaders' "Copenhagen Accord" was only noted by the conference, not adopted. It did leave two things that lasted: the idea of keeping warming below 2 °C, and a promise from rich countries to mobilise $100 billion a year in climate finance for developing countries by 2020.

"Copenhagen" is still shorthand for a summit that raises hopes and then falls short.

## Paris, 2015: the deal we have now

The **Paris Agreement** was adopted at [COP21](/events/cop21/) in December 2015 and came into force less than a year later. It flipped Kyoto's model around. Instead of targets imposed on a few countries, every country sets its own, and is expected to make them stronger over time.

Its central goal is to hold warming "well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels" and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C ([UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement)). Our [Paris Agreement page](/paris-agreement/) covers where it stands now, including the US withdrawal and how each country is doing.

## The numbers and terms you'll hear

**1.5 °C and 2 °C.** These are limits on the rise in global average temperature compared with "pre-industrial" times, usually measured as the average for 1850 to 1900. Half a degree sounds small, but the difference between 1.5 °C and 2 °C means noticeably more heatwaves, floods, crop losses and damage to coral reefs.

**"We've passed 1.5 °C."** 2024 was the first calendar year to be about 1.55 °C above pre-industrial levels ([WMO](https://wmo.int/news/media-centre/wmo-confirms-2024-warmest-year-record-about-155degc-above-pre-industrial-level)). That doesn't mean the Paris goal has been broken yet, because the goal refers to warming averaged over decades. But on current trends that long-term average will pass 1.5 °C too, at least for a while.

**NDCs.** Nationally determined contributions are each country's own climate plan under Paris: how far it will cut emissions, by when, and how it will adapt. They are due every five years and each new one is supposed to be more ambitious than the last ([UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement/nationally-determined-contributions-ndcs)). When you read that the world's plans "don't add up", it means all the NDCs added together still lead to more than 1.5 °C.

**Net zero.** A country or company is at net zero when any greenhouse gases it still releases are balanced by the same amount being taken out of the atmosphere, for example by forests or carbon capture. It doesn't mean zero emissions, which is why the plans behind a net zero promise matter more than the date. Scientists estimate that keeping to 1.5 °C needs global CO₂ emissions to reach net zero around 2050.

**Global stocktake.** Every five years, countries look at whether their combined efforts are on track for the Paris goals. The first stocktake ended at [COP28](/events/cop28/) in Dubai in 2023, and the second began at [COP31](/events/cop31/) in 2026 ([UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/topics/global-stocktake)). Its findings are meant to shape the next round of NDCs.

**IPCC.** The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change is the UN body that assesses the science. It doesn't do its own research. It brings together thousands of published studies into reports that governments approve line by line. When you hear "the science says", it usually means the IPCC.

**Climate finance.** Money from richer countries to help poorer ones cut emissions and cope with a changing climate. It is one of the most argued-over topics at every COP.

**Loss and damage.** Help for countries already suffering harm from climate change that can't be adapted to, such as land lost to the sea. A fund for it was agreed at [COP27](/events/cop27/) in 2022.

**Just transition.** Making sure the move away from fossil fuels is fair to the workers and communities who depend on them now.

## Why it matters to you

These agreements can feel remote, but they work through national laws, council plans and company decisions, which is where ordinary people have a say. The NDCs are only as strong as the pressure behind them at home.
